One thing I see far too many times on IG these days are reels made by people who are using a hook like, "Here's how I built a brand in 1 hour," and then the entire showcase is just a relatively plain visual identity for what could be a brand. Some of it is great work and sure, hooks have to be slightly exaggerated to win the algorithm but don't be fooled by their logic. Branding is a cosmetic choice: a font, a logo, a color palette you pick. If you treat your brand like a coat of paint, then you're missing the fact that a brand isn't what you look like. It's the premium that people are willing to pay for because of what you make them feel.

As the good old Marty says, "A brand is not a logo. It's a gut feeling." When you have a brand you know it because customers are picking you even though it may seem completely irrational. This actually leads me to another statement by Marty: "A brand isn't what you say it is. It's what they say it is."

Let me try to exemplify this. My brother loves telling me that I could get some generic Android phone for half the price of an iPhone and it's just as good but do I listen? No, because, like every other iPhone user, I'm not just looking for specs. Apple's branding got the best of me and I'm not afraid to admit it. Don't get me wrong, Apple makes great products but they've just decided that great products should come at a premium and their brand gives them the power to charge that. You see I'm paying for the design, the sleekness, the prestige, and the immediate recognition that comes with the Apple logo. Sure I'll admit it: it's an identity plate but I love a great brand and I'm willing to pay the premium. So what? It's a matter of taste.

Branding works because it bypasses the wallet and hits the ego, convincing the buyer that owning your product actually makes them a bit more. When your brand is a utility you're just one discount away from being replaced but when your brand is an identity you've become irreplaceable. The goal of building an iconic brand is to move your product away from the logic part of the brain to the limbic system, where why always beats how much. That takes much more than a visual identity. Every great brand is trying to tell a story and in every great story there's a villain. Netflix once said that its biggest competitor was sleep, which sounds ridiculous until you understand what they were actually competing for.

There's an interview where Reed Hastings, the CEO of Netflix, was asked about companies like Amazon Prime and HBO. His view was basically that the entertainment market is enormous and the real constraint is your time. You see, every night Netflix is competing with whatever else you could be doing: working out, going out, scrolling, gaming, coding, and eventually going to bed. Sleep became this perfect villain of their story.

There's a really useful branding lesson in that, which is naming the villain, because your customer has something that they want and usually something is standing in the way. The interesting villain is often much bigger than the competitor selling something similar to whatever you're selling.

Being very good is one of the worst places for a brand to be because nobody complains about very good but nobody calls a friend about it either. Very good is the restaurant you enjoyed and then completely forgot about two weeks later. You do not want your brand to be very good.

Seth Godin talks about being remarkable instead of being very good and he specifically states, "Remarkable doesn't mean remarkable to you. It means remarkable to me. Am I going to make a remark about it? If not then you are average and average is for losers." Remarkable has a different job than very good. The word literally means worth making a remark about and sometimes that means making choices that people actively dislike.

Gentle Monster understands this perfectly. Their stores have these surreal installations with futuristic vibes, industrial machinery, and this giant animatronic dog. It doesn't exactly feel like an eyewear store when you walk in. I bet you thought that was an ad, huh? Sadly it wasn't. Some people will think it's absolutely ridiculous but you know what? That's perfectly fine because plenty of others will film it and post it online and send it to their friends and have them talk about it because they just have to see this place.

That reaction is worth far more than universal approval. A remarkable product gives people a story whereas a very good product just doesn't. If you're looking to improve the experience around your product, ask yourself what would make someone text their friend about this.
